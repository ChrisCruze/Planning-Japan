What makes this monument genuinely special isn't just its massive size, though at over forty-three feet tall, it is breathtaking. It's the history. Originally, this giant bronze statue was housed inside a massive wooden temple hall. But in the late fifteenth century, a massive tsunami swept through the city and carried the entire building away. The building was destroyed, but the Buddha remained sitting peacefully in the open air. And he has sat there, under the sun, rain, and snow, ever since. As a travel advisor, I always tell people that Kamakura is the perfect antidote to the neon intensity of Tokyo. It grounds you. ### Section 6: Getting There and Insider Tips

Getting to Kamakura from Tokyo is a breeze, but the local transit requires a little know-how. From Kamakura Station, the group will need to transfer to the Enoden Line—a beloved, vintage-style tram that runs right through people's backyards—and ride it to Hase Station. From Hase, it's about a ten-minute walk to the Kotoku-in Temple.

Here is my biggest insider tip for this day, and it echoes Chris's notes perfectly: **Bring cash.** While Tokyo is becoming increasingly card-friendly, traditional spots like Kamakura still run on physical yen. You will need coins for the temple entrance, the 50-yen fee to go inside the Buddha, and the countless street food stalls you'll pass along the way. Your IC cards—like Suica or Pasmo—will cover all the train fares, so make sure those are loaded up before leaving Tokyo to avoid the long ticket machine lines. And regarding etiquette: remember that this is a functioning Buddhist temple. Keep voices low, don't eat while walking on the temple grounds, and soak in the atmosphere respectfully.
