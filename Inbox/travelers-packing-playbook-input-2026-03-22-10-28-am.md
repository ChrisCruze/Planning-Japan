# The Smartest Thing You Can Pack Is Empty Space

## Seasoned Japan travelers know the real game isn't what you bring — it's how much room you leave for what you're about to find.

> **Notes:** Takkyubin shipping from your hotel in Hanzomon to Narita/Haneda typically takes same-day to next-day and can be arranged at the hotel front desk or at any konbini. # Leave It Home — These Are the Biggest Packing Mistakes for Japan

## Some things feel essential at home and become dead weight on the plane. Know the cuts before you zip the bag.

- **Shampoo, conditioner, body wash:** Every hotel in Japan provides these. Your Nest Hotel will have them in the room. These are dead weight that also eat up your TSA liquids allowance.
- **A hairdryer:** Every hotel has one. Always.
- **A travel umbrella (initially):** Japan sells the most beautiful, cheap, and sturdy convenience store umbrellas (¥500–800 at any konbini) the moment it rains. Don't pack one — just buy one there if needed and leave it as a souvenir.
- **Heavy toiletry bag:** Consolidate hard. Toner, serum, SPF, one moisturizer — decant into travel sizes or buy on arrival. Drugstore chains like Matsumoto Kiyoshi and Sundrug near train stations carry international and Japanese skincare at aggressive prices.
- **Books or physical entertainment:** Cherry blossom season Tokyo will fill every waking moment. Your phone camera will work overtime. There is genuinely no dead time on this trip that requires a paperback. If you want to read on the flight, download to your phone.
- **A universal power adapter:** Japan uses Type A plugs — the same flat two-prong configuration used in the US. Your American devices plug directly into Japanese outlets with zero adapter needed. The one exception: any device with a grounded three-prong plug needs a simple (small) adapter, but most phone chargers and electronics don't.

> **Notes:** The one toiletry exception — Western-style deodorant. Japanese deodorant options skew heavily toward light spray/mist formats that won't cut it if you're active. Pack your stick for the full trip.

# Tokyo in April Is Sneaky — Layer or Get Caught in the Cold After Dark

## Cherry blossom season is genuinely beautiful but meteorologically unpredictable — pack for a 30°F temperature swing within the same day.

- **The temperature trap:** Days can hit 68°F and feel like spring; evenings drop to 48–52°F and feel like late fall. If you're at a hanami picnic until 8PM or walking back from teamLab at night, a single t-shirt will leave you miserable.
- **The layering formula:** Base layer (t-shirt) + mid layer (lightweight long-sleeve or henley) + outer layer (bomber, overshirt, or packable jacket). Three layers that compress into nothing. This beats one heavy jacket for versatility across the whole day.
- **Rain is likely at least once:** Early April in Tokyo has a moderate chance of rain. Rather than packing a rain jacket, the konbini umbrella purchase is the move — it's part of the local ritual. If you prefer a packable rain layer, a thin hooded windbreaker doubles as both.
- **DisneySea specific:** April 8 outdoor park — dress in genuine layers. Theme park days start warm from physical activity and get cold when you stop moving at night.

> **Notes:** Mt. Fuji day is likely to be the coldest single day of the trip — altitude change is real. Even in April, the Fuji area can be 10–15°F colder than central Tokyo. If you have a packable puffer that compresses small, that's the one day it earns its place in the bag.

# A Few Things You Wouldn't Think to Pack That Japan Will Actually Reward You For

## Some of the best "packing" decisions for Japan aren't clothing — they're small items that signal cultural awareness and make daily life smoother.

- **A small handkerchief or fast-dry pocket towel:** Many Japanese public restrooms don't provide paper towels for hand drying — just air dryers, and sometimes nothing at all. A small pocket handkerchief (or the compact quick-dry towels sold in travel sections) becomes essential within the first hour.
- **A coin purse or wallet with a coin section:** Japan uses coins heavily — ¥100 and ¥500 coins are common denominations for vending machines, coin lockers at train stations, and small purchases. American wallets don't accommodate this well. A small snap-top coin pouch keeps the chaos organized.
- **A tote bag or foldable shopping bag:** Japanese stores often charge for plastic bags (or don't offer them). Having a compact, reusable tote folded in your daypack makes you ready for any market, convenience store run, or souvenir haul.
- **Business cards if relevant:** In Japanese professional culture, exchanging business cards (meishi) is a ritual. If you have any professional context on this trip — even loosely — having a few cards handles those moments gracefully.

> **Notes:** Coin lockers at major train stations (Shinjuku, Shibuya, Tokyo Station) are extremely useful for stashing bags during day trips. They accept IC cards (Suica) now at most locations — no need for exact coin change.
