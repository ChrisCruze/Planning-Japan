## Day 2 — Saturday, April 4: Sky Hop Bus Orientation Day

### Getting There & Back from Nest Hotel

The Sky Hop Bus hub is the **Marunouchi Mitsubishi Building**, directly outside the Marunouchi Exit of Tokyo Station. From Hanzomon Station, take the Hanzomon Line to Otemachi (1 stop, 2 minutes), then walk 5 minutes to the Mitsubishi Building. Alternatively, it's a 20-minute walk from the hotel through the Imperial Palace area — a scenic route that passes Chidorigafuchi (cherry blossoms) along the way.

### What Is the Sky Hop Bus?

The Sky Hop Bus is an open-top, double-decker sightseeing bus that operates across **3 color-coded routes and 19 total stops** throughout Tokyo. Your ticket gives you unlimited hop-on, hop-off access for 12 hours across all three routes. Buses run approximately every 20–45 minutes on each route, and every seat comes with a **7-language audio guide** (GPS-synced) covering historical and cultural commentary. Ponchos are provided if it rains; in the event of thunderstorms, operations may be suspended.

### The Three Routes

**Red Route — Asakusa & Tokyo Skytree (Tradition & History)**

This route has the most stops and the highest frequency (approximately every hour). It takes you through Tokyo's historic heart. Key stops and what you'll find:

- **Marunouchi Mitsubishi Building (Start):** Hub stop adjacent to Tokyo Station's iconic redbrick Marunouchi facade. The Daimaru department store and its legendary depachika (basement food hall) are steps away.
- **Ryogoku:** The sumo district. You'll pass near the Kokugikan (National Sumo Arena) and the Edo-Tokyo Museum. If it's morning, you might spot sumo wrestlers walking the streets in their robes.
- **Tokyo Skytree:** Japan's tallest structure at 634 meters. Two observation decks (350m Tembo Deck and 450m Tembo Galleria) offer jaw-dropping views. The Solamachi shopping complex at the base has 300+ stores, including a Pokemon Center and a Studio Ghibli store. Observation deck admission runs ¥2,100–3,100 (~$14–20). Advance booking recommended for time slots.
- **Asakusa:** Home to Senso-ji Temple (Tokyo's oldest, founded 645 AD), Nakamise Shopping Street, and Kaminarimon (Thunder Gate). The area is electric with street food vendors, souvenir shops, and a distinctly old-Tokyo atmosphere.
- **Ueno:** Ueno Park — one of Tokyo's best cherry blossom spots with 800+ trees. The Tokyo National Museum, the zoo, and several art museums cluster here.
- **Akihabara:** "Electric Town" — multi-story electronics shops, retro game arcades, manga stores, figurine emporiums, and maid cafes. Even if anime isn't your thing, the sensory overload is pure Tokyo.

**Blue Route — Tokyo Tower, Tsukiji & Ginza (Waterfront & Icons)**

This route arcs through the bay area and connects several of your trip's confirmed stops:

- **Tokyo Tower:** Your Day 7 destination, but seeing it from the bus gives you a preview and helps you orient for later. Zojoji Temple sits at its base.
- **Tsukiji Outer Market:** The bustling remnant of the world-famous fish market. Fresh sushi, tamagoyaki (sweet rolled omelet), grilled seafood skewers, and local snacks. Perfect for a 60–90 minute food crawl.
- **Ginza:** Tokyo's most upscale shopping district. Mitsukoshi department store's depachika is a culinary treasure trove. The Chuo-dori boulevard is pedestrianized on weekends.
- **Odaiba:** Futuristic waterfront entertainment district. Rainbow Bridge views, teamLab facilities, Fuji Television headquarters, and the life-size Unicorn Gundam statue.

**Green Route — Shinjuku & Shibuya (Modern Energy)**

This route takes you through Tokyo's most kinetic neighborhoods:

- **Shinjuku:** World's busiest train station. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building offers a **free** 45th-floor observation deck with panoramic views (including Mt. Fuji on clear days). Omoide Yokocho (Memory Lane) is a warren of tiny yakitori and ramen stalls under the train tracks. Golden Gai — 200+ micro-bars in six narrow alleys — comes alive at night.
- **Shibuya:** The iconic Scramble Crossing (up to 3,000 people per light change), the Hachiko dog statue, and Shibuya Sky — a rooftop observation deck at 230 meters with no glass barriers. Sunset time slots sell out during cherry blossom season; book 1–2 weeks ahead at shibuya-sky.com.
- **Meiji Shrine & Harajuku:** The bus passes near Meiji Shrine (Shinto shrine set in 170 acres of forest) and Takeshita Street (teen fashion, crepes, photo booths, and wild street style).

### Optimization Strategy

Start with the **Red Route** first thing in the morning (9 AM). Hop off at Asakusa for Senso-ji and Nakamise (allow 60–90 minutes), then ride to Akihabara if arcades interest anyone (45–60 minutes). After lunch, switch to the **Green Route** for Shibuya Crossing and Shibuya Sky at sunset (~6:15 PM in April). The Blue Route can be done as a scenic ride-through without hopping off, or save Tokyo Tower and Tsukiji for their dedicated days.

**Pro tip from reviews:** Buses can fill up on weekends — if you hop off, allow extra time to catch the next one (50+ minute gaps are common). Pick up at any stop; no need to return to the hub.

## Day 3 — Sunday, April 5: Kamakura Day Trip

### Getting There & Back from Nest Hotel

The guided tour departs from a central Tokyo meeting point (typically Shinjuku or Tokyo Station area) at approximately **7:00–8:30 AM** and runs ~9 hours. The tour is via GetYourGuide (Ref: GYGX7NL393M3, ~$64/pp). The group will be transported by the tour operator. If you needed to go independently, Kamakura is accessible via JR Yokosuka Line from Tokyo Station (~55 minutes, ~¥950) to Kamakura Station, then the Enoden Railway to Hase Station (3 stops).

### Where Is Kamakura?

Kamakura sits on the Pacific coast of Kanagawa Prefecture, approximately **50 km southwest of central Tokyo**. It's nestled in a natural amphitheater of forested hills that open to the sea — a geography that was strategically chosen centuries ago because the mountains provided natural fortification from invasion.

### The Significance, Symbolism & Story

**Kamakura was Japan's first military capital.** In 1185, the samurai warlord Minamoto no Yoritomo defeated his rivals and established the Kamakura Shogunate — Japan's first military government. For nearly 150 years (1185–1333), this small coastal city was the political center of the entire nation. This is where the concept of the "shogun" ruling Japan truly solidified, and where the samurai warrior class rose to supreme power. The shift from Kyoto's imperial court to Kamakura's military rule fundamentally reshaped Japanese society, culture, and governance for the next 700 years.

**The Great Buddha (Daibutsu) — Kamakura's Centerpiece:**

The Great Buddha at Kotoku-in temple is a monumental bronze statue of **Amida Buddha (Amitabha)** — the Buddha of Infinite Light. Here's what makes it extraordinary:

- **Cast in 1252**, during the height of the Kamakura period. It is over **770 years old**.
- Standing **13.35 meters tall (43.8 feet)** including its base and weighing approximately **121 tonnes**, it is Japan's second-largest monumental Buddha (after Nara's Todaiji).
- It represents **Pure Land Buddhism** — the belief that through devotion to Amida Buddha, all beings, regardless of social standing, can be reborn in the Western Paradise. This was revolutionary: unlike earlier Buddhist sects that required years of monastic study, Pure Land offered salvation to warriors, farmers, and common people alike. It democratized spiritual hope.
- The statue was originally housed inside a massive wooden temple hall. But a **tsunami in 1498** (from the Nankai earthquake) washed the building away entirely. Since then — for over **500 years** — the Great Buddha has sat in the open air, exposed to typhoons, earthquakes, and wars. This resilience has made the statue a cultural symbol of **endurance and inner peace** in the face of destruction.
- The statue is **hollow** — visitors can enter the interior for a small fee to see the casting marks from the 13th century.
- It is a **designated National Treasure of Japan** (since 1958) and one of 22 historic sites in Kamakura's UNESCO World Heritage proposal.
- The hands rest in the **dhyana mudra** (meditation gesture) — the specific Kamakura variation called *mida no join* — symbolizing deep contemplation and the promise of rebirth.

**What You'll Feel Standing There:**

Unlike the Nara Buddha (which sits inside an enormous hall), the Kamakura Buddha faces you directly under the open sky. The effect is startlingly intimate. You see weather-worn bronze, traces of original gold leaf that catch afternoon sunlight on the right cheek, and a face of profound calm that has watched over this valley since the year Europeans were building Gothic cathedrals.

### Beyond the Great Buddha — What Else to See

- **Hase-dera Temple:** A hilltop temple with panoramic ocean views, a massive golden Kannon statue (goddess of mercy), and a cave carved with Buddhist figurines. 5-minute walk from Kotoku-in.
- **Tsurugaoka Hachimangu Shrine:** Kamakura's most important Shinto shrine, founded by Minamoto Yoritomo himself in 1191. A long approach avenue (Wakamiya Oji) lined with cherry trees leads to the hilltop shrine.
- **Komachi-dori Street:** A lively shopping street near Kamakura Station packed with souvenir shops, matcha sweets, and street food (try the purple sweet potato soft serve).
- **Yuigahama Beach:** If the tour has free time, the coast offers a completely different energy — salty ocean air, surfers, and a relaxed vibe that feels nothing like Tokyo.

### Practical Notes

- **Bring cash:** Many shops and food stalls in Kamakura are cash-only.
- **Wear sneakers:** Temple paths involve uneven stone steps, gravel, and hills.
- **Sunday crowds:** Expect domestic weekend visitors. Kamakura is a popular day trip for Tokyo residents.
- **Dinner tip:** Keep it simple that night. The group will return tired. The hotel neighborhood (Hanzomon) has quiet izakayas within walking distance.

## Day 4 — Monday, April 6: Mt. Fuji Full-Day Tour

### Getting There & Back from Nest Hotel

The guided tour departs from central Tokyo (typically Shinjuku area) at approximately **8:00 AM** and returns by early evening. The tour is via GetYourGuide (~$65/pp). Transportation is provided by the operator — typically a coach bus.

### What Makes Mt. Fuji So Extraordinary

**Mt. Fuji (Fujisan) is not just a mountain. It is Japan's soul made visible.**

At **3,776 meters (12,389 feet)**, it is Japan's tallest peak — a near-perfectly symmetrical stratovolcano that rises alone above the landscape, visible from over 100 km away on clear days (including from Tokyo). It is a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** (designated 2013), recognized not as a natural wonder but as a **cultural** heritage site — a distinction that underscores what Fuji means to Japan beyond geology.

**Sacred Mountain:**

- Fujisan has been worshipped since ancient times. The Japanese traditionally saw the divine in all aspects of nature, and Fuji's towering form and terrifying eruptions made it a dwelling place of gods.
- The Shinto deity **Konohanasakuya-hime** (Tree Blossom Princess) is enshrined at Sengen shrines at both the base and summit. The mountain fuses Shinto and Buddhist worship — pilgrims have climbed toward the summit seeking spiritual purification for over 1,000 years.
- In the 17th century, the **Fuji-ko** pilgrimage movement exploded in popularity. Ordinary citizens — not just monks — began making sacred ascents. The practice became so fervent that the Tokugawa Shogunate eventually banned the sect.

**Artistic Icon:**

- Mt. Fuji has inspired art for over 1,200 years, appearing in Japan's oldest poetry anthology (*Man'yoshu*, 8th century) and the world's oldest prose narrative (*The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter*).
- Katsushika Hokusai's *Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji* (1830s) — including "The Great Wave off Kanagawa" — made the mountain a global icon. These prints influenced Van Gogh, Monet, and even the composer Debussy.
- Fuji appears on Japan's ¥1,000 bill and is perhaps the most painted mountain in human history.

**Geological Power:**

- Fuji is approximately **100,000 years old** — actually three volcanoes layered on top of each other over millennia.
- The last eruption was in **1707** (the Hoei eruption), which lasted 16 days and sent volcanic ash all the way to Tokyo (Edo). The secondary peak, Hoeizan, is the scar from that eruption.
- It is classified as an **active volcano** and is closely monitored. Japan has detailed evacuation plans for a future eruption.
- The eruptions created the **Fuji Five Lakes** (Kawaguchiko, Yamanakako, Saiko, Shojiko, and Motosuko) at the mountain's base, which are among the day tour's stops.

**What the Tour Covers:**

Most full-day tours from Tokyo visit the Fuji Five Lakes area (particularly Lake Kawaguchiko), the 5th Station at ~2,300m elevation (the highest point accessible by vehicle — views permitting), outlet shopping at Gotemba Premium Outlets, and scenic photo stops. Weather is unpredictable — Fuji reveals itself fully on clear days, but cloud cover is common. The tour experience is rewarding regardless: the lakes, the forests, and the crisp mountain air create a complete contrast from Tokyo.

### Practical Notes

- **Bring a jacket:** The Fuji area is significantly colder than Tokyo. At the 5th Station, temperatures can be 10–15°C lower. In April, expect near-freezing conditions at altitude.
- **Bring your passport:** Required for tax-free shopping at outlets.
- **Bring snacks and a portable charger:** Long bus day = phone battery killer + limited food stops.
- **Weather mindset:** You cannot control Fuji's visibility. If clouds roll in, enjoy the lakes and the scenery. If the mountain reveals itself, it will be one of the most memorable moments of the trip.
- **Evening:** Keep it light when you return to Tokyo. Consider Tokyo Ramen Street at Tokyo Station (8 acclaimed ramen shops in a single basement corridor) for an easy dinner.

### What Is Shimokitazawa?

Shimokitazawa (affectionately "Shimokita") is Tokyo's **bohemian neighborhood** — a laid-back enclave of narrow, winding streets packed with vintage clothing shops, independent cafes, live music venues, curry restaurants, record stores, and tiny izakayas. It is the antithesis of Shibuya's neon intensity.

Key characteristics:
- **Over 200 vintage/thrift clothing stores** — Japan's secondhand culture means items are often in pristine condition. Standouts include New York Joe Exchange, Flamingo, and TreFacStyle (luxury resale).
- **Shimokita is Japan's soup curry capital** — Hokkaido-style curry with generous vegetable toppings. Top spots include Rojiura Curry Samurai, Magic Spice, and Ponipirika.
- **Live music houses:** Shelter, Shimokitazawa Three, and ERA host indie and alternative acts nightly.
- **Shirohige's Cream Puff Factory:** A bakery founded by Hayao Miyazaki's (Studio Ghibli) sister-in-law, selling Totoro-shaped cream puffs. Queues form daily.
- **Reload & Bonus Track:** New-wave shopping complexes built on former railway lines — hip restaurants, a Tsutaya bookstore, co-working spaces, and open-air terraces.

### The Significance of Tokyo Tower

**Tokyo Tower is more than an observation deck — it is the symbol of Japan's postwar rebirth.**

Built in **1958**, just 13 years after the devastation of World War II, Tokyo Tower represented Japan's re-emergence as a modern, forward-looking nation. Standing at **333 meters**, it was intentionally designed to surpass the **Eiffel Tower** (324 meters) — a deliberate statement of ambition. It was the **tallest structure in the world** at its completion.

Key facts:
- **220,000 craftsmen** built it in just **18 months**, largely by hand. A remarkable feat of engineering and collective will.
- The steel was partially sourced from **decommissioned American tanks** from the Korean War — transforming instruments of war into a beacon of peace and progress.
- Its signature red-and-white paint scheme is actually an **aviation safety requirement** (12 alternating bands), not a design choice — but it has become one of Tokyo's most beloved visual signatures.
- It functions as a **broadcast antenna** (the original purpose for its construction), though Tokyo Skytree now handles most digital broadcasting.
- Two observation decks: the **Main Deck** at 150m and the **Top Deck** at 250m.
- Foot Town, the 4-story complex at the base, includes a One Piece anime theme park, restaurants, and shops.

**The Tokyo Tower + Zojoji Temple Frame:**

The most iconic photograph in this part of Tokyo is Tokyo Tower rising behind **Zojoji Temple** — ancient Buddhism framed against Space Age steel. This juxtaposition captures something essential about Japan: the seamless coexistence of deep tradition and relentless modernity.

### What's Nearby — The Shiba Park/Zojoji Cluster

**Zojoji Temple (増上寺):**

A 600-year-old Buddhist temple sitting directly at the foot of Tokyo Tower. Founded in 1393, it became the Tokugawa family temple — the ruling dynasty of Japan for 260 years. Six of the 15 Tokugawa shoguns are buried here.

Highlights:
- **Sangedatsumon Gate:** Built in 1622, it is the **oldest wooden building in Tokyo** and the only structure to survive WWII bombing. The name means "gate of deliverance from three earthly states: greed, anger, and foolishness." Walking through it is said to free you from these passions.
- **Sentai Kosodate Jizo:** A garden of 1,000 small stone statues dressed in knitted clothing and decorated with toys — representing the souls of miscarried, stillborn, and aborted children. Parents choose one to dress and pray for their child's soul. Poignant and deeply moving.
- **Tokugawa Mausoleum:** The burial site of six shoguns, open for ¥500 admission.
- **Treasure Gallery:** Featuring 500 Arhat paintings by Kano Kazunobu and a 1/10 scale model of the destroyed Daitokuin Mausoleum. ¥700 admission.

**Shiba Park:**

One of Japan's first public parks (designated 1873). Built on former Zojoji grounds. In spring, over 200 cherry trees bloom here, and in autumn, "Maple Valley" puts on a red-leaf display. Tokyo Tower is visible from every angle in the park. A tree planted by **US President Ulysses S. Grant** in 1870 still stands near the main hall.

**Atago Shrine:**

A small hilltop shrine known for its extremely steep stone staircase ("Success Steps" — climbing them is said to bring career success). Prior to skyscrapers, this was Tokyo's highest natural point and served as a fire lookout.

**teamLab Borderless (Azabudai Hills):**

Located a 15–20 minute walk from Tokyo Tower in the Azabudai Hills complex. If the guys are looking for a Day 7 activity while the women do the kimono experience, this is a natural pairing: 75+ digital artworks that flow between rooms with no boundaries, including EN TEA HOUSE where digital flowers bloom from your tea. ¥3,800 (~$25). Advance timed-entry booking required at teamlab.art.
